You are coding a single case from the Delhi High Court commercial docket for an empirical legal-research dataset. You do TWO things, at the CASE level, for the whole case (not per order):
  (1) decide whether this is an intellectual-property (IP) dispute at all, and
  (2) if it is, identify which IP rights the plaintiff actually ASSERTS.
You output ONE JSON object. Do NOT assume the case is an IP case — decide it from the evidence in the orders.

You receive all orders of one case in chronological order, separated by `=== ORDER N ===` headers (each with order_filename and order_date). Output valid JSON and nothing else.

==============================================================
STEP 1 — IS THIS AN IP CASE?  (decide first; do not assume)
==============================================================

Read the orders and classify ip_status as exactly one of "yes", "no", "unclear".

- "yes" — there is AFFIRMATIVE evidence this is an IP dispute. Any ONE suffices:
    * a claim, prayer, or judicial finding on an IP right (infringement of a trade mark / copyright / patent / design / GI; passing off; personality rights; trade-secret / confidential-information / source-code misappropriation), OR
    * unambiguous IP-suit markers: "registered trade mark", registration numbers for the plaintiff's suing mark, "infringement", "deceptively similar", a Local Commissioner appointed to seize infringing goods, John Doe / dynamic injunction / website-blocking relief, or linkage to an IP-Division matter (e.g. "C.O.(COMM.IPD-TM)", "IPD-PAT", "IPD-CR").

- "no" — there is AFFIRMATIVE evidence it is NOT an IP dispute: the suit is plainly something else (recovery of money / contract, defamation, oppression-mismanagement, arbitration, tenancy, a pure commercial claim) and no IP right is in issue anywhere in the orders.

- "unclear" — you cannot tell from the supplied orders. Typically every supplied order is procedural (summons, exemptions, condonation, pleadings management, mediation, adjournments) and NONE states the plaint's subject, any prayer, or any finding. Do NOT default to "yes" because the suit is a CS(COMM) number or because of the parties' industry — absence of the plaint/opening order means "unclear", not "yes".

Put a short verbatim quote (or a one-line reason for "no"/"unclear") in ip_evidence.

If ip_status is "no" or "unclear": set rights_asserted = [], rights_evidence = {}, and STOP — do not code rights. Only proceed to Step 2 when ip_status = "yes".

==============================================================
STEP 2 — WHICH RIGHTS ARE ASSERTED?  (only if ip_status = "yes")
==============================================================

A right is ASSERTED only when the text shows (1) the plaintiff SEEKS A REMEDY on that basis, OR (2) the court makes a FINDING on it. A right is NOT asserted when the text only describes what the plaintiff OWNS (portfolio recital), or uses a word in passing. Owning a copyright registration is not a copyright claim; "patent-filed software" is not a patent claim. If you cannot quote a prayer or a finding, do not code the flag.

Anchor on the FIRST SUBSTANTIVE ORDER (the one that sets out the plaint / prayers / dispute). Combine early orders if needed. rights_asserted is one case-level array.

CLOSED LIST — per-flag test (code true only if you can answer YES and quote it):
- "trademark" — relief for / finding of infringement of a REGISTERED mark. Look for "registered trade mark/mark", registration no. of the suing mark. Trade dress/packaging/shape = trademark ONLY if asserted registered.
- "passing_off" — passing off pleaded or found; OR (where registration is not stated) relief on goodwill/reputation/deception/get-up/prior-user grounds. Captures all common-law misrepresentation incl. unregistered marks, trade dress, get-up, unregistered designs framed as passing off, character merchandising, domain/website impersonation.
- "copyright" — relief for / finding of copyright INFRINGEMENT (artistic/literary/software work). Owning a copyright registration is NOT enough.
- "patent" — relief for / finding of patent infringement, or the Patents Act as the basis. "patent-filed" descriptors do not count.
- "designs" — REGISTERED-design infringement (Designs Act). Unregistered designs framed as passing off = passing_off.
- "GI" — geographical-indication claim (GI Act, s.16/22).
- "personality_rights" — publicity / personality-rights claim (name, image, voice, likeness).
- "other_ip" — trade secrets, confidential information, source-code misappropriation, plant varieties, semiconductor layouts.

TRADEMARK vs PASSING_OFF (registration decides):
- Registered mark, infringement only → trademark true, passing_off FALSE.
- Registered mark AND passing off pleaded together → BOTH true (modal Delhi HC pattern; do not drop the passing_off limb).
- Unregistered mark / trade dress, passing off → trademark FALSE, passing_off true.
- Registration not stated → passing_off true if the language fits; trademark only if registration is affirmatively indicated.
- Plaintiff owns a registration but sues only on common-law rights → passing_off true; trademark only if a registered-mark infringement claim is actually made.

Analysis
12. Assessment of Confusion in Trademarks
Through the years, courts have formulated and propounded various tests for a
comparative assessment of trademarks in infringement/passing off cases, to
somehow rationalize the inherent subjectivity in this assessment. Some of these
tests have been a result of pure judicial innovation, and some have nuanced an
already existing formulation. Though it would be impossible to give an
exhaustive list of these tests which are scattered all over trademark
jurisprudence, an attempt is being made under to provide an illustrative list of
such tests, with their sources, and then to categorize the core elements of
assessment into recognizable factors.
13. Illustrative list of tests for assessment of trademark confusion
- The “Pianotist Test” articulated by Parker J. in Pianotist Co. Ltd.’s
application, 23 RPC 774 at 777, was cited with approval by the Hon’ble
Supreme Court in Amritdhara Pharmacy v. Satya Deo Gupta, 1962 SCC
OnLine SC 13;
- The consumer test of “man of average intelligence with imperfect
I.A. 23362/2023 in CS(COMM) 843/2023 Page 39/65
recollection” was articulated by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Corn
Products Refining Co. v. Shangrila Food Products Ltd., 1959 SCC
OnLine SC 11;
- In K.R. Chinna Krishna Chettiar v. Shri Ambal & Co., (1969) 2 SCC 131,
the Hon’ble Supreme Court propounded in favour of phonetic similarity,;
- The Supreme Court laid down the “added matter test” in Kaviraj Pandit
Durga Dutt Sharma v. Navaratna Pharmaceutical Laboratories, AIR
1965 SC 980;
- This Court clarified the interplay between “dominant mark rule” and
“anti-dissection” rule in South India Beverages Pvt. Ltd. v. General Mills
Marketing, 2014 SCC OnLine Del 1953;
- The test of “initial interest confusion” was referred to by this court in
Baker Hughes Ltd. v. Hiroo Khushalani, 1998 SCC OnLine Del 481;
- This Court in Shree Nath Heritage Liquor Pvt. Ltd. v. Allied Blender &
Distillers Pvt. Ltd., 2015 SCC OnLine Del 10164, cited with approval the
“Sleekcraft Factors”, the “Polaroid Factors” and the “DuPont
Factors”, which form part of American trademark jurisprudence;
- In the case of S. Syed Mohideen v. P. Sulochana Bai, (2016) 2 SCC 683,
the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that a prior user has a superior right
over a registered owner;
- The Supreme Court laid down the “Cadila Principles” for determining
I.A. 23362/2023 in CS(COMM) 843/2023 Page 40/65
likelihood of confusion in passing off cases, in Cadila Healthcare Ltd.
(supra);
- This Court in Pankaj Goel v. Dabur India Ltd., 2008 SCC OnLine Del
1744, held that onus remains on the defendant to show significant
business turnover, of other marks on the Register;
- This Court in AMPM Fashions (P) Ltd. v. Akash Anil Mehta, 2021 SCC
OnLine Del 4945, highlighted the relevance of an inter-dependent and
holistic approach to balancing various considerations between marks and
consequently laid down the “Global Appreciation Test”;
- The Hon’ble Supreme Court in Midas Hygiene Industries (P) Ltd. v.
Sudhir Bhatia, (2004) 3 SCC 90, held that mere delay in bringing an
action is not sufficient to defeat grant of injunction;
- This Court in Vardhman Buildtech Pvt. Ltd. v. Vardhman Properties Ltd.,
2016 SCC OnLine Del 4738, in relation to part of a mark, held that
registration does not confer any exclusive right;
- The Hon’ble Supreme Court in Registrar of Trade Marks v. Ashok
Chandra Rakhit Ltd., 1955 SCC OnLine SC 12, held that registration of
the mark may not grant any statutory right or monopoly with respect to
that part;
- This Court in Kirorimal Kashiram Marketing & Agencies Pvt. Ltd. v. Sita
Chawal Udyog Mill, 2010 SCC OnLine Del 2933, held that arbitrary
adoption of a word mark with respect to a product with which it has no
I.A. 23362/2023 in CS(COMM) 843/2023 Page 41/65
correlation is entitled to a very high degree of protection;
- This Court in Automatic Electric Ltd. v. R.K. Dhawan & Anr., 1999 SCC
OnLine Del, held that a party was estopped from claiming lack of

- This Court in P.P. Jewellers (P) Ltd. v. P.P. Buildwell (P) Ltd., 2010 SCC

- In the decision of Hindustan Pencils Pvt Ltd. v. India Stationery Products

- This Court in Schering Corpn. v. Alkem Laboratories Ltd., 2008 SCC

- In the decision of Nandhini Deluxe v. Karnataka Coop. Milk Producers

of goods, if not using the said trademark in respect of certain goods (being

14. Factors for assessment of trade mark confusion – The Guideposts
These and various other tests and factors have been propounded from time to
time by various courts. Rather than trundling through these innumerable
parameters, factors and nuances scattered all over the jurisprudence, this Court
has attempted to categorize them into intelligible, thematic silos. Each category
strings together and collates factors, any or all of which may be needed for
assessment of trademark confusion. Needless to state these factors are not
exhaustive, but may serve as useful guideposts when embarking on a journey
of trade mark assessment. These categories and factors are as under
A. Strength of the marks
A.1 Registration and user details
A.2 History of enforcement
A.3 Evidence of use
A.4 Sales and advertising
A.5 Portfolio of goods where mark is used
A.6 Disclaimers in registrations
A.7 Well-known marks
A.8 Other similar/identical marks in use/ registered/common to trade
B. Similarity between marks
B.1 As to word and/or device
I.A. 23362/2023 in CS(COMM) 843/2023 Page 43/65
B.2 As to appearance, layout, get-up, trade dress
B.3 As to representation in commerce
B.4 As to phonetic similarity
B.5 As to overall impression
B.6 As to connotation
B.7 Extent of similarity/dissimilarity
B.9 Anti-dissection rule
B.9 Dominant mark rule
B.10 Part of mark
C. Proximity of goods and services
